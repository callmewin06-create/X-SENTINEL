## 5. Experimental Setup
### 5.1. Datasets and Data Audit
We evaluated the implemented detector on static Windows Portable Executable (PE) feature vectors from EMBER2018 and an EMBER2024 extension. EMBER2018 uses the 2,381-dimensional V2 schema; its prepared source contains 600,000 labeled training records and 200,000 test records. Unlabeled training records were excluded. EMBER2024 uses the 2,568-dimensional V3 schema. All six official PE archives were audited, while only the approved sample budget of 232,500 training and 20,000 test records was materialized. These experiments evaluate feature-vector modifications rather than modified executable files.
Within each dataset, we froze seven disjoint roles using split seed 17. Fit, selection, development, confirmation, reference and calibration were drawn from official training data; final evaluation used official test data. All classification roles were balanced, whereas reference and calibration contained benign samples only. Table 3 reports the counts used for each dataset. This train-derived D1 design differs from the archived workflow that reserved reference/calibration samples from test.

Source SHA256 identifiers and array checksums were retained. The split procedure checked source uniqueness, train/test separation and disjointness between roles, and excluded locally known previously observed IDs. However, missing source IDs in an external handover prevent a claim of complete historical non-observation. For EMBER2024, official PE-scope records with is_pe=0 were retained when upstream extraction produced 2,568 finite values; the flag and all feature values were preserved. Schema-specific mapping assigned each coordinate to Structural, Behavioral/API or Metadata/string. Behavioral features represent static imports/exports, not measured runtime behavior.
### 5.2. Models and Attack Construction
We trained binary LightGBM classifiers on the 200,000-record fit pool. Main clean and poisoned models used 500 boosting rounds; the full detector added three clean view classifiers with 150 rounds each. Training used 64 leaves, learning rate 0.05, max_bin 255, feature_fraction 0.9, bagging_fraction 0.9 and bagging_freq 1. Deterministic, column-wise training and four CPU threads were enabled. Main-model classification used a fixed malware-probability threshold of 0.5.
We considered concentrated Structural triggers with two features, spread Structural triggers with ten features, and cross-three-view triggers with eight features per view. The first two families used feature-restricted profiles. Cross-three-view was explicitly a vector stress test; independently overwriting its 24 coordinates does not demonstrate a realizable PE modification. The spread family therefore tests within-Structural dispersion rather than a Metadata-only scenario.
For each trigger, a random subset of benign fit samples was modified while retaining benign labels and the original training-set size. Poisoning rates were 0.5%, 1% and 2% of all 200,000 fit samples, corresponding to 1,000, 2,000 and 4,000 modified benign records. These are 1%, 2% and 4% of the benign fit pool. Seeds 17, 29 and 43 produced 27 family/rate/seed configurations per dataset. Clean-label construction and feature selection were local adaptations of explanation-guided attacks; they were not treated as exact reproductions of the authors' original algorithm.

### 5.3. Development and Confirmation Protocol
Feature/value candidates were computed from the selection pool using a clean fit model and locked before development predictions. Development compared legacy_rare with signed_shap_conditioned over 54 configurations per dataset. A single selector was chosen for both datasets according to the declared rule: prioritize the number of strong-and-stealthy runs, then mean paired gain, with a predefined final tie-break. This resulted in legacy_rare, which passed 24 of 54 development configurations across the two datasets, compared with 8 of 54 for signed_shap_conditioned. Confirmation and final observations were not used for selector choice.
An attack passed the internal screen when it had at least 1,000 eligible malware samples, eligible ASR of at least 50%, gain over the same-trigger clean-model control of at least 20 percentage points, and a clean accuracy drop strictly below 0.5 percentage points. A confirmed configuration passed both development and the independent confirmation pool. These are protocol-specific heuristics rather than universal attack criteria.
Confirmation and final evaluation adopted the exact frozen development model and trigger without retraining on held-out data. Weak or unconfirmed configurations remained in the final matrix. Three clean view models were trained once per dataset/seed for the full variant, using clean fit data. The detector did not receive the clean control classifier, attack manifest or poisoning labels; these were available only to construction/evaluation modules.
### 5.4. Evaluation Metrics and Statistics
Let E be the set of final malware correctly classified as malicious by the clean model at threshold 0.5. Eligible ASR is the proportion of E classified as benign by the poisoned model after trigger insertion. We also applied the same trigger to the clean model to measure direct evasion independent of poisoning. Attack gain is the difference between these two eligible evasion rates. Clean accuracy, malware true-positive rate and benign false-positive rate were evaluated separately on unmodified final data.
Detection AUROC compares scores for all 10,000 triggered final malware records against 10,000 unmodified final benign records. Our operating-point recall is calculated on successful eligible attacks: members of E that the poisoned model already misclassifies as benign. This denominator differs from recall on all triggered malware. Detector FPR is the fraction of final benign inputs flagged. Post-defense ASR retains the original eligible denominator and counts successful attacks left unflagged; it simulates blocking by the alert rule and does not imply actual file quarantine.
Reference scores were used for empirical-rank normalization, and each score/variant was calibrated separately on 2,000 benign training records. The target calibration FPR was 1%. Thresholds were order statistics with strict score > threshold flagging, allowing at most 20 calibration exceedances and conservative behavior under ties. Thresholds were frozen before final evaluation. The final FPR was measured independently and could exceed the target.
Saved rate metrics include 95% Wilson intervals. Full-minus-reduced differences use 1,000 within-run paired bootstrap resamples, preserving matched observations. Source IDs are reused across model seeds, so configurations were not pooled into independent samples. Aggregate tables below report equal-weight descriptive means by run, not pooled confidence intervals or significance claims. Individual examples retain their original denominators and uncertainty.
### 5.5. Experiments E0-E5
E1 assessed attack viability before interpreting detector performance. E2 compared TADR, tabular STRIP, view contribution, reduced/full agreement and their fused scores. STRIP used 50 convex vector mixtures with alpha=0.5 and content/seed-derived sampling. Primary fusion combined M3 view_mass and the corresponding M4 through equal-weight benign-reference empirical ranks. M5 was disabled in the primary protocol.
E3 examined matched detections using saved per-input scores, rather than AUROC differences alone. E4 compared reduced and full while holding the main model, reference, calibration and final IDs constant. E5 measured final benign FPR and warmed per-input latency. The completed primary evidence supports a partial TADR distribution comparison under the poisoned model; a complete four-group clean/poisoned E0 comparison, primary M5 ablation, dedicated D0 budget sweep and verified rare/packed-benign evaluation are not included in the current results.
### 5.6. Runtime and Reproducibility
Primary runs used the local Windows workflow with four native training/prediction threads. The recorded environment uses LightGBM 4.6.0 and NumPy 1.26.4. Batch evaluation used 128 inputs per scoring batch, but latency measurements invoked one input at a time. Per configuration, 100 inputs were sampled without replacement from final benign and triggered-malware vectors. Each variant received a warmup prediction, and measurement order alternated between variants on the same samples.
Timings include the complete detector prediction call, including STRIP; they exclude upload and feature extraction. They are not isolated SHAP timings or end-to-end application measurements. Model-byte counts include the main classifier for reduced and the main plus three view classifiers for full. Whole-process peak memory was recorded but cannot be attributed separately to variants. Configurations, source snapshots, candidate locks, split IDs, model hashes, threshold bundles and paired predictions were preserved. A separate review loaded all 108 bundles and reconstructed the metrics of the 54 completed final configurations. Historical pilot results are excluded from the primary tables below.

## 6. Results
### 6.1. Attack Viability E1
All 27 declared final configurations completed on each dataset, with no failed or unsupported cells. Nevertheless, only 12 of 27 configurations per dataset passed both attack screens. EMBER2018 contributed three concentrated and nine spread configurations; EMBER2024 contributed three spread and nine cross-three-view configurations. Spread Structural triggers on EMBER2018 showed mean ASR 73.78% against a 1.06% clean-trigger control, supporting a substantial poisoning-associated effect. In contrast, cross-three-view triggers on EMBER2018 reached ASR 99.82%, but the clean-trigger control was already 89.38%. The average separation was consequently much smaller than the screen's 20-point gain requirement, and no configuration in that family passed both screens. High ASR alone therefore did not establish a qualifying backdoor attack.
EMBER2024 cross-three-view configurations had mean ASR 99.51% and a 21.53% control, with all nine passing both screens. Concentrated configurations on EMBER2024 did not pass both screens and had mean ASR 28.92%. Clean final accuracy drops across all 54 configurations ranged from -0.095 to +0.380 percentage points; negative drops represent small improvements. This indicates limited observed clean-accuracy degradation, but it does not eliminate the direct-evasion confound or prove binary feasibility.

EMBER2018 spread ASR was 69.45% with Wilson 95% CI [68.52%, 70.36%]; EMBER2024 cross ASR was 99.87% with CI [99.77%, 99.92%]. These final estimates are distinct from the development/confirmation screen decisions.

Full increased mean recall from 35.75% to 42.94% on EMBER2018 and from 12.08% to 19.11% on EMBER2024. Mean FPR also increased from 0.52% to 0.78% and from 0.76% to 0.90%, respectively. The full variant's mean AUROC was lower on EMBER2018 despite its higher recall, illustrating that ranking and thresholded detection are different objectives.
For EMBER2024, full left post-defense ASR at 76.19% compared with the 88.85% unblocked mean ASR of the confirmed cohort. Most successful attacks therefore remained unflagged. STRIP had zero successful-eligible recall in all 24 confirmed runs across both datasets; TADR also had zero in the 12 confirmed EMBER2024 runs. Their low AUROCs and zero recalls were retained without score reversal or threshold retuning. Dataset-level averages should not be read as direct cross-dataset superiority because the confirmed family compositions differ.

We reconstructed matched catch/miss counts from the preserved predictions of two illustrative configurations: EMBER2018 spread and EMBER2024 cross, both at seed 17 and 1% poisoning. For EMBER2018 spread, TADR detected none of the 6,701 successful attacks, while M4-F detected 2,506 (37.40%). The two methods missed 4,195. For EMBER2024 cross, M4-F detected 812 of 9,638 (8.42%) that TADR missed, while 8,826 were missed by both. These matched counts establish input-level complementarity for the stated configurations, while also showing substantial remaining misses.
The fused full detector flagged 3,165 of 6,701 successful attacks in the EMBER2018 example and 808 of 9,638 in the EMBER2024 example. Fusion therefore cannot be equated with a union of component detections: its separate reference normalization and calibration change which inputs exceed threshold. These examples do not establish the same degree of complementarity for every run. A systematic raw-feature or view-SHAP case explanation is not presented from these saved score arrays.
### 6.5. Ablation E4
The completed ablation is the paired reduced-versus-full comparison. Both variants used identical poisoned main models, trusted-benign pools and final IDs; full additionally used three clean view classifiers and the probability-gap M4. Table 9 reports original within-run bootstrap differences for the same two configurations. Units are percentage points, including the AUROC differences.

On EMBER2018 spread, full increased recall by 6.00 points, but decreased AUROC by 1.05 points and increased FPR by 0.22 points. On EMBER2024 cross, full increased AUROC by 14.57 points and recall by 8.38 points, while decreasing FPR by 0.21 points. The bootstrap intervals describe paired uncertainty within these configurations, not independent replication across all seeds or a universal full-variant advantage.
This comparison changes both access to view models and the corresponding M4 definition; it is not an isolation of one component's causal contribution. No primary M5 experiment was performed. Accordingly, the contribution of semantic plausibility and RQ3 remain unanswered in the current study.
### 6.6. False Positives and Latency E5
The calibrated 1% target did not imply a 1% final FPR. Across all 27 runs per dataset, X-R final FPR ranged from 0.32% to 0.83% on EMBER2018 and was summarized separately from confirmed-only means. For TADR on EMBER2018, final FPR ranged from 0.23% to 2.78%. In the representative spread configuration, full FPR was 0.74% (74/10,000; Wilson 95% CI [0.59%, 0.93%]), while reduced FPR was 0.52% (52/10,000; CI [0.40%, 0.68%]). These values illustrate calibration-to-final variation without redefining thresholds.

Mean warmed detector latency was 28.24/28.65 ms for EMBER2018 reduced/full and 22.93/23.38 ms for EMBER2024. These whole-detector measurements exceeded the 10 ms target. Since STRIP was included even when its detections were not used in fusion, the small latency difference between variants does not isolate the cost of the additional view classifiers. Full used approximately 1.90 times the classifier bytes of reduced. Shared-process memory peaks cannot establish variant-specific RAM differences, and no cross-hardware performance claim follows from the local timings.
The current primary FPR evidence concerns the balanced held-out benign pool. Dedicated rare/packed-benign cohorts and a primary D0 budget sweep were not evaluated here, so no subgroup robustness or D0 operating-point claim is made. The reported results therefore establish a bounded comparison on static vectors, with observed detection gains in some cohorts and substantial remaining attack success, rather than comprehensive backdoor protection.
